# Design and architecture

The prototype is an isometric woodland survival and production game. It is rendered in 3D with code-built low-poly models: natural woodland, modern DIY buildings and no fantasy styling. The 2D art comparison boards in `docs/art/` are historical samples; only the interface icons still use that illustrated art.

## Player loop

The intended fresh-start loop starts with the steel axe the player carries: gather wood, stone and berries, knap a stone chisel, carve planks and a mallet, build a crafting bench and make crates there, build a trading post and sell crates of berries for a steel pickaxe. Then mine iron and coal, smelt iron in a basic furnace, build and operate a reinforced furnace (`reinforced`) to make steel, hammer it into steel parts at the anvil and make steel tools. Pack a steel tool into a crate and sell it through the trading post and use the proceeds to hire and assign a worker. There are no iron tools; iron bars only go into buildings and steel. The 10–15 minute duration is a pacing target, subject to hands-on UI and exported-build review.

Food, water, the day/night cycle, shelter, wages, and wolf encounters support the production loop. Wolves come out of open ground just beyond every player's sight radius, so none appears on screen. A chasing wolf (`Wolf.Chase` above zero) gallops at `wolfChase` times `walkSpeed`, which data validation keeps above walking and below `sprintSpeed`; stalking, creeping and backing off stay at the slower trot. Campfire light repels wolves (`campfireHours` sets how long one log burns), though a wolf already chasing the player follows into it for a few seconds. A torch in hand (`SaveState.Torch`, burn seconds left) burns as long as one log, only while `SaveState.TorchLit`; lighting takes `World.KindleSeconds` (`SaveState.TorchKindling`). Swings share the axe cooldown, flare the light (`World.TorchFlare`) and put the torch out after `World.WildSwings` within `World.WildWindow` seconds. While it burns and fewer than `World.PackSize` wolves are near, an unscared wolf creeps in at a third of its speed and can bite; a swing aimed within about 45° of a wolf in `World.ScareRange` sets `Wolf.Scared`, and a scared wolf keeps its bearing at `World.StalkDistance` without biting or leaping. Spawns slow down but the cap rises to six, and once the pack is complete swings no longer scare and the wolves attack. A direct torch hit sets `Wolf.Flee`; inside a cabin (`SaveState.Sheltered`) the player is hidden and wolves back off at once. At half health or less, an unprotected player can be pinned by a leaping wolf; the pinned state (`SaveState.Mauled`) blocks movement and actions, drains health while the pack feeds, and makes the following death strip food from the recovery box. Killed wolves leave a `wolf_corpse` resource that yields raw meat, which the stone furnace cooks into edible meat. Sleeping in a cabin advances time toward dawn and is refused if hunger or thirst would run out first; `hungerHours` and `thirstHours` in `data/game.json` set how many in-game hours a full bar lasts. Holding Shift while moving sprints at `sprintSpeed` times the walking speed (`PlayerState.Sprinting`, sent by guests with their walk), and hunger and thirst drain `sprintDrain` times as fast meanwhile; under 15% food or drink (`GameData.LowNeed`) the player cannot sprint and the screen edges pulse red. Death moves carried items to a recovery box and respawns the player at the clearing after a 30-second timer. Recovery boxes are never automation storage and disappear once empty.

Fog of war is simulation state. `World.Look` runs every tick: it marks tiles within `SightRadius` of the player in `SaveState.Explored` and replaces `SaveState.Remembered` entries in sight with snapshots of the live things there. `World.Known` is what the view draws and picks: live things in sight, snapshots elsewhere, nothing never seen. Snapshots are updated in place and keep only the look: no inventory, fire, heat or work in progress. The view picks against what it draws but only returns things that still exist. `WorldView` hides wolves, workers and carts outside sight and puts one overlay shader on every mesh: black on unexplored tiles, a grey wash on explored ones, clear inside the sight circle.

## Implemented rules

- An anvil only progresses while someone hammers it: the player standing within reach without walking or gathering, or a paid assigned worker hammering at the station. A smith stays with a running batch and fetches the next load once it is done.
- The basic furnace accepts wood or mined coal for fuel. The reinforced furnace (`reinforced`) burns coal only, and steel only progresses while the heat is at or above the clear bar (`World.SteelHeat`, shown as 1450 °C through `World.Celsius`); a batch pauses below it, keeps burning one coal from its fuel slot per batch length while cold, and resumes when reheated, with no failure. Once an active crew has the forge hot, it never falls below the bar while they are on duty.
- Steel recipes consume iron bars and mined coal. Charcoal production is not implemented.
- Tools carry a `tool` kind (axe, pickaxe, hammer, chisel, rake, mortar), a `speed` and a `tier` (0 wood or stone, 1 iron, 2 steel) in `data/game.json`; `World.BestTool` picks the fastest carried one at or above a tier. Recipes name the tool they need, the lowest `tier` that will do, and whether they can be made by hand (`hand`). So a steel chisel does everything a stone chisel does, faster, while tin cans need a steel hammer and the mortar a steel chisel. Hand recipes queue in `SaveState.Crafting`, take their inputs at once, run at base speed times the tool's speed while the player carries it, and give the materials back when cancelled. The bench runs at `benchSpeed` times the speed of the tool in its own chisel or hammer slot (`Thing.Tools`), and like the anvil only while someone works it.
- Items carry what eating or drinking one restores (`hunger`, `thirst`, `health`) and a `food` flag. Food is what wolves eat and compost will take, cooked or not; an item that restores nothing, such as raw meat, wheat or dough, only says it needs cooking.
- Tin sits at the foot of a third cliff further inland: like iron, it needs a pickaxe. Tin ore smelts into tin bars at the stone furnace, and a tin bar makes 4 cans at the bench.
- A farm plot (`farm`, `src/Farm.cs`) is a zone like a pile and snaps with piles. `farm` in `data/game.json` sets what sows it, how many days the wheat grows, how many working days the harvest takes and what it yields. `Thing.Stage` is `World.FarmFallow`, `FarmGrowing` or `FarmRipe`; `Thing.Progress` counts growth seconds, then harvest work. A fallow plot sows itself from its own inventory. `World.CropLook` gives the look: bare, soil, straws, green plants or wheat. The player harvests through `World.Gather` and assigned workers through `World.FarmWork`, both adding to the one progress. The yield goes into the plot's `Thing.Output`; Nothing moves seed back into the sowing inventory on its own. A worker on Farm harvests; one on Haul first moves seed from the output into the sowing inventory, then carries out the rest and fetches what the next sowing lacks. `DepositFor` ranks compost last for food, so harvests and berries go to storage first.
- A recipe with `fuel` burns one wood or coal per batch from the station's one-slot fuel box (`Thing.Burner`); `Thing.Fuel` follows what was last loaded, `World.FuelFits` limits the reinforced furnace to coal, and `World.BatchCost` lists materials only. Haulers keep the burner topped up (`Worker.Fueling`). `World.SlotKinds` names the tool slots a station has (`Thing.Tools`): a chisel and a hammer at the bench, a mortar at the cooking table (`cooktable`). A recipe that needs a tool at such a station waits for it to be slotted, runs at that tool's speed, and, at the cooking table, only while someone works it (`World.Worked(Thing)`): the mortar's recipes are worked, while jam, stew and dough cook unattended and their worker only hauls. The canning station (`cannery`) seals a filling and a `tin_can` into a canned good over fuel.
- A tool station (`toolstation`) is storage for tools only. `World.ToolKind` names the tool a resource needs: an axe for trees, a pickaxe for deposits, a rake for leaves; stone, berries and loose coal need none. A worker without one walks to the nearest station holding one, takes the best (`Worker.Tool`), and gathers at that tool's speed. With none available they wait, show what they need and say it once in the corner message. `World.ReturnTool` walks a tool the job no longer needs, or any tool at dusk, back to the nearest station with room; with no station the worker keeps it. A dismissed worker's tool goes into their recovery box. Oaks drop a patch of leaves beside the trunk (`World.LeafPatch`), refilled every dawn.
- Piles (`pile`) are 3×3 yards (`World.IsZone`, `ZoneSize`) that do not block walking. `World.Snap` puts a new yard on the grid of the nearest yard within reach, and `ZoneBlock` lets yards touch but not overlap. A pile takes part in automation like a chest. `World.PileFilters` sets its input and output filters each tick from what it holds: its one resource, or nothing while empty. `HoldBlock` refuses a second resource, including for manual transfers. A compost pile (`Thing.Compost`) instead takes `Compostable` items (leaves, grass and anything with `food`), gives `fertilizer`, and turns `CompostBatch` of them into one fertilizer each in-game hour. It keeps the player's filters rather than setting its own, and `Thing.SkipFood` keeps workers from delivering food to it.
- Every tool and its parts stay reachable: a chisel is always 2 stone away, a mallet is a wood part, neither is sellable, and the first pickaxe or a lost axe is bought with coins from berry crates. `CheckNoToolDeadlocks` verifies this.
- A wood crate holds `crateCapacity` (12) units. A picked-up crate becomes an item `crate_<n>` whose contents live in `SaveState.Crates`; an empty one is the stackable item `crate`. Crates are manual storage only. Loose water bottles take a slot each; only a crate stacks them. Putting a packed crate into a placed crate asks for confirmation and, if confirmed, ends the run in a black hole with a reload or main-menu choice.
- The market only accepts packed crates of the goods in `market.sells`; a sale pays for the contents unit by unit plus the crate. `market.buys` lists what the village sells and in what lot: steel tools singly, canned food and water as crates of 12, wheat seeds and fertilizer as crates of 4.
- Inventories are slot grids with stacks of 64; a worker carries one stack of up to 5.
- In `market.sells` each good's first `steady` units sell at the full price, each crate's worth past them takes `drop` off it, and a day restores what selling `daily` units takes (`World.Demand`, which runs above 1 by the steady amount); buying costs a fixed 1.25× the base price. A cart trip takes 30 seconds; purchases settle on return into the trading post's market output (`Thing.Output`), with overflow in a box beside it. Sale coins fill the trading post's one-stack wage slot (`Thing.Wages`, coins only) first and the output after; dawn wages and settled strikes draw on every wage slot before the payer's backpack.
- The price sheet shows `SaveState.Prices`, a snapshot of sale prices taken when a cart returns or when a worker on the trading post's deliveries job comes back from a price errand (`Worker.Away`). Trades always use the live price, so the sheet can be out of date.
- `hireOffers` in `data/game.json` lists hiring deals; each worker keeps the daily wage of the deal they were hired on (`Worker.Wage`). A picked-clean berry bush fruits again after `berryRegrowHours`. Prices, wages and demand follow the rules in [ECONOMY.md](ECONOMY.md).
- Workers keep a schedule. At the `lunchHour` in `data/game.json` they break for one in-game hour and sit in a circle around the campfire closest to the whole crew (`World.LunchFire`), every other one cross-legged on the ground and the rest on a rock, or where they stand without one. As dusk begins (`GameData.DuskStarts`, a tenth of a day before night) they walk to the nearest trading post, or the clearing without one, then walk a cart's trip down the valley over half a trade trip while fading out (`Worker.Commute`), and sign out (`Worker.Home`). At dawn they walk back the same way, fading in, and return to work. Assignments and carried items are kept throughout. A crew at lunch or signed out does not hold the forge at the clear bar or hammer an anvil, and cannot run price errands; an errand already under way finishes first.
- A mine (`src/Mine.cs`, `mine` in `data/game.json`) is marked free and snaps to `World.MineSpot`, directly in front of an ore cliff, one per cliff; each portal follows its own cliff face, its tunnel runs into the rock, and its rails point outward toward the valley. Older valley saves move only the mine cart anchor to this aligned site while retaining stock, stages and worker assignments; a continuous outcrop extends from the mountain foot, blocks walking with a matching footprint (`World.InSpur`), and holds the tunnel, which `WorldModels.Mine` darkens step by step to black. `Thing.Stage` counts finished stages: dug by a worker with a pickaxe (`World.MineDug`), supplied with `mine.materials` (`World.MineSupplied`), and fitted by a worker with a hammer (`World.MineOpen`); the two worker stages take `stageWorkdays` of `GameData.WorkdaySeconds`. Workers fetch the pickaxe or hammer a stage needs from a tool station, like any other job. Up to `mine.workers` miners work underground (`WorkerWorkState.Underground`, hidden by the view), each adding one ore and one stone to the mine's two-slot `Thing.Output` every `mine.seconds`. Mining stops while either slot holds a full stack; the first free miner then carries the whole stack to storage (`Worker.Bag` grows to the stack size until emptied), and with no storage the mine reports once that it is full.
- The end goals replace the old ending: a working mine on every ore in `World.Cliffs`, and `goals.profitDays` consecutive days of at least `goals.dailyProfit` profit. `SaveState.DayRevenue` collects returned sale coins; at dawn the wages charged are taken off and the result joins `SaveState.Profits`. Reaching the streak sets the `profit_week` milestone, which stays reached. The starter goal chain stays as the tutorial, and the pause menu lists both.
- Workers are tracked individually and hold duties (`Worker.Duties`): a place and one of the jobs `World.Jobs` offers there, such as gather, craft, haul, bellows, farm, build, mine, deliveries, fuel, stock or tend. `World.SetDuty` adds or drops one; `Worker.Assignment` is the place being worked now, and `World.NextPlace` moves on to the worker's next place when the current one has nothing to do or after a delivery, so one worker can cover several places in turn. Only one worker ever pumps a reinforced furnace: of those with the bellows job the first pumps and the rest haul if they also hold haul; a lone worker with both alternates. Stock brings tools from storage to a tool station; tend fetches what a compost pile's filters take from storage and carries its fertilizer out; deliveries runs price errands and carries the trading post's mail, other than coins, to storage.
- Chests have separate item allowlists for input and output. Empty lists allow all items. Automated deposits prefer a chest with a matching input filter, then a nearby unrestricted chest. Automated withdrawals respect output filters. Manual transfers ignore those filters.
- Multiplayer is host-authoritative. `SaveState.Players` holds one `PlayerState` per player and the top-level player fields of `SaveState` pass through to `SaveState.Actor`, so saves keep the host's player where single player always had it. Player actions run in the acting player's context (`World.As`); in a guest's copy, `World.Forwarded` sends each action to the host as a `Command` instead of changing the copy. Guests walk locally and send their position, gather target and held item; a host-made move such as a respawn bumps `PlayerState.Warp` so it wins over the guest's own position. Sleep needs every player sheltered, wolves each hunt one player (`Wolf.Target`), and messages reach the acting player unless they concern everyone.
- Pollution, vampires, and technology beyond steel are outside this POC.

## Code and data

The project targets Godot 4.7.2 .NET with C#. The `World` simulation and `SaveState` are in `src/World.cs`; inventories and their filter-aware transfers are in `src/Inventory.cs`; tunable items, buildings, recipes, trade values, and time values are loaded from `data/game.json` by `src/GameData.cs`. `src/Game.cs` owns UI, input, save/load, and the main game loop. `src/Style.cs` holds the shared enamel camp-gear look (speckled enamel boards with a cream rim, beige wells, cream cups, brick for what is active or wanted, Fredoka and Nunito) and the heat, day and cart-road dials, `src/Panels.cs` the shared panel parts (header buttons, the filter window, the process row with its fuel slot and side outputs, job buttons and their worker lists), `src/Grid.cs` draws the icon-cell grids, `src/ItemSlot.cs` carries drag and drop between them, `src/Machine.cs` draws station input and output slots, `src/Crafting.cs` the crafting menu and the crafting bench panel, `src/BlackHole.cs` the crate-in-crate warning, collapse shader and game over screen, `src/Market.cs` the trading post market, hiring offers and price sheet, `src/Crew.cs` the crew cards and worker panel, `src/Guide.cs` the field guide topics, `src/Needs.cs` the HUD health, hunger and thirst vials, the bank and the red damage edges, `src/Ending.cs` the one-time ending screen, `src/StartMenu.cs` the start menu with its save slot, load and options screens, and `src/Hotbar.cs` implements nine saved item/plan references, assignment, icons and use. Health, hunger and thirst are HUD icons; coins are a backpack item that stacks to 1000. Every page that works with the backpack shows it in one place, a drawer above the hotbar (`Game.BagGrid`), with the stacks that page takes rimmed in brick and the rest faded; the side panel only shows the building and sizes itself to what it holds. `src/WorldView.cs` owns the 3D scene, orthographic camera, picking, lighting and world animation; `src/WorldScene.cs` adds ground dressing, particles and the animation review; `src/WorldModels.cs`, `src/ActorRig.cs` and `src/ModelKit.cs` build the models. `src/Sounds.cs` plays sound effects. `src/Players.cs` holds per-player state, the command dispatcher and guest adoption; `src/Net.cs` runs the ENet connection, world updates and guest saves. An update holds only what changed since the last one the guest confirmed: the host keeps the world of each recent update as one JSON entry per thing, worker, wolf, trade cart and player plus two shared entries, and sends changed fields of changed entries. The welcome is a guest's first update, with the fog uncovered so far. Updates go unreliably on their own channel, so a lost one never delays the next; the whole world after a long stall, or a change too large for one packet, goes reliably on a third channel, so messages and actions never wait behind it. Guests merge entries into their copy in place, so panels and the view keep their objects. Each action carries where the guest stood, so the host checks reach from there rather than from a position still gliding in, and leaving asks the host for the guest's player before the line closes. Factorio instead runs the same deterministic simulation on every machine and sends only inputs (lockstep); this simulation uses floats and frame-length steps, so it is not deterministic, and with hundreds of objects the deltas stay small; `src/NetMenu.cs` the host and join menus. `scenes/Main.tscn` is the project entry point and references `src/Game.cs`.

Keep balance and recipe values in `data/game.json` so they can be adjusted without changing simulation code. Keep rendering and input separate from state transitions so headless checks can exercise gameplay rules without relying on screenshots. `tests/EconomyChecks.cs` measures real workers through a day and checks the economy rules. Simulation checks live in `tests/SimulationChecks.cs` and cover filters, worker routing, furnace work, trade, wages, recovery after death, saves, and a material-driven progression run.

Every world object is a low-poly model assembled from Godot primitive meshes in code; meshes and materials are cached by parameters so repeated objects share resources. One tile is one world unit and an adult is about 1.65 units tall. The camera is orthographic with a 30° pitch, matching the old 2:1 isometric footprint and actor scale. Picking, the hover ring and canopy fading use the same per-kind vertical hit cylinders (`WorldModels.Hits`), so selection matches what is drawn. A tree crown only wins a click when nothing else is under the cursor, so objects behind a canopy stay selectable. Trees use three variants chosen by object id, so saves need no new fields. Fading swaps in cached alpha-blended copies of a model's materials, so faded trees still share a handful of materials. `assets/` keeps only the illustrated icon atlases and SVG toolbar icons.

Actor animation is driven by gameplay state. `ActorAnimation` maps state to an action and returns keyframed joint angles (`Pose`) for any time; `ActorRig` applies them to a hierarchy of hips, torso, head, shoulders, elbows, thighs and knees, lifting the hips so the lower foot stays on the ground. The player's carried tool selects its matching action and the tool shown in hand; workers animate according to movement and an explicit transient `WorkerWorkState`, not localized or presentation status text. The transient state is excluded from saves and recalculated on the next simulation tick. Manual bellows pumping and the unarmed punch trigger one loop of their action. Each work action has an impact moment that shakes its target and spawns chips, leaves, splashes or sparks. Pausing freezes animation and effects.

Sound effects are one-shot MP3s in `assets/sounds`; `x_1.mp3`, `x_2.mp3` and so on are variants of one sound `x`. Each play picks a variant other than the previous one with a small random pitch and volume change. Audibility follows the camera, so zooming out lets the player hear further: a sound on screen plays at full volume, one just past the screen edge is faint, and it fades to silence 5 tiles beyond the edge (14 for a distant wolf growl). The simulation names discrete events through `World.Sound` (a fire catching, a wolf bite, a cart's payment) and stays silent while sleep fast-forwards. The view plays sounds tied to animation timing: footsteps by ground type, axe and anvil impacts, storage lids and the cabin door. `Game.Act` gives manual inventory moves and spending a sound by item kind when the action made none of its own. Music in `assets/music` crossfades over four seconds between the day music and the night music when night falls or dawn breaks, including a sleep that skips to morning. The day plays its tracks one after another and the night loops one. Each keeps its place for the running session, so music longer than its part of the day carries on next time, and it wraps back to the first track at the end. The options screen sets the music volume, stored in the settings file.

## Development and verification

Use the repository launcher from its root:

```sh
./build.py play
./build.py test
./build.py build
```

`play` sets up Godot 4.7.2 .NET and the .NET 10 SDK locally when missing. `test` runs the simulation checks with a 120-second limit and requires `SELF-TEST PASS`, followed by Godot input/UI checks requiring `UI-TEST PASS`, then a host and a guest process on this machine requiring `NET-HOST PASS` and `NET-GUEST PASS`; the guest joins through a relay in `build.py` that adds about 80 ms of delay and 5% packet loss each way. `build` runs both, exports a Linux executable, smoke-tests it, and packages the executable, game pack and runtime into a tar archive. Tool downloads and build output remain under ignored project-local directories.

The project targets Linux x86_64 and Godot's Forward+ (Vulkan) renderer, whose device selection prefers a discrete GPU. First-time setup requires internet access; interactive play requires a supported graphics driver. The export is a runnable prototype. The world is a fixed 216-tile valley defined in `src/Terrain.cs`: sea past the coast, mountain ranges beyond a valley half-width that narrows inland, and a lake whose shore function bounds both walking and the drawn water. `World.Scatter` places resources with a seeded generator, trees more densely toward the mountains; saves from earlier maps are refused, and workers use grid detours around water and buildings.
